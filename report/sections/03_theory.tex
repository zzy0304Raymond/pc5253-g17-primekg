\section{Theory: Survival as Redundancy}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{One principle}
\label{sec:theory-principle}

Consider an object protected by $X$ redundant units. It survives damage if at
least one unit survives. If each unit survives independently with probability
$q$, the object fails with probability $(1-q)^X$. Averaging over objects, with
$G_X(z)=\sum_x \Pr(X=x)\,z^x$ the probability generating function (PGF) of $X$,
gives
\begin{equation}
\text{surviving fraction}=1-G_X(1-q).
\label{eq:principle}
\end{equation}
The low end of the distribution of $X$ controls this expression: objects with
$X=1$ fail with probability $1-q$, while the tail contributes little.
Equation~\eqref{eq:principle} applies to our three observables with different
units, unit survival probabilities and redundancy distributions
(Table~\ref{tab:principle}).

Far from the percolation threshold this is elementary: a node survives if it
keeps an edge, a query if it keeps one intact route. Its use is to identify the
controlling quantity, the low end of each redundancy distribution, and to give an
exact baseline; the physics lies where it fails
(Sections~\ref{sec:theory-chance}--\ref{sec:theory-rinf}).

\subsection{Connectivity: percolation and its local limit}
\label{sec:theory-gf}

The course derived the giant component of a random graph with degree
distribution $p_k$ from generating functions \cite{newman2001random}. Let
$g_0(z)=\sum_k p_k z^k$, let $g_1(z)=g_0'(z)/g_0'(1)$, and let $u$ be the
probability that an edge does not lead to the giant component. For edge
retention $p$ and node retention $\phi$,
\begin{align}
u&=1-p+p\,g_1(u), & S_{\mathrm{bond}}&=1-g_0(u), \label{eq:gf-bond}\\
u&=1-\phi+\phi\,g_1(u), & S_{\mathrm{site}}&=\phi\,\bigl[1-g_0(u)\bigr]. \label{eq:gf-site}
\end{align}
A giant component exists only for $p$ above the Molloy--Reed threshold
\cite{molloy1995critical}
\begin{equation}
p_c=\frac{\langle k\rangle}{\langle k^2\rangle-\langle k\rangle},
\label{eq:pc}
\end{equation}
which is 0.0053 for the sample (and for the full graph) and 0.0062 for the sample's PPI layer alone.

The damage we study lies far from this threshold, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gf-bond}
then simplifies. An edge leads to a node that has, on average, $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle-1\approx188$ further edges, so almost every surviving edge leads into the giant component. Then $g_1(u)\approx0$, $u\approx1-p$, and
\begin{equation}
S\approx1-g_0(1-p),
\label{eq:s-local}
\end{equation}
the fraction of nodes that keep at least one edge, which is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:principle} with edges as units. On the sample it matches the
simulation to within 0.001 for $f\le0.8$; collective percolation behavior
appears only beyond $f\approx0.95$ (at $f=0.99$ the simulated $S$ is 0.112, the
full equation gives 0.126 and Eq.~\eqref{eq:s-local} 0.291). Over most of the
damage range, connectivity survives for a local reason.

\subsection{Two-hop retrieval: an exact reliability polynomial}
\label{sec:theory-r2}

A two-hop query with $m$ shared proteins $x_1,\dots,x_m$ has $m$ routes
$d\!-\!x_i\!-\!y$. A route needs both of its edges (logical AND), and the query
needs any one route (logical OR). This is a series--parallel network, the
structure Moore and Shannon used to build reliable circuits from unreliable
relays \cite{moore1956reliable}. Routes through different proteins share no edge,
and every entity pair carries one relation (Section~\ref{sec:data-graphs}). When
each edge survives independently with probability $p$, the routes therefore
fail independently, and
\begin{equation}
R_2(p)=1-G_m(1-p^2).
\label{eq:r2}
\end{equation}
For blind node removal, the drug and the disease must survive, and each route
needs only its protein:
\begin{equation}
R_2^{\mathrm{node}}(\phi)=\phi^2\bigl[1-G_m(1-\phi)\bigr].
\label{eq:r2-node}
\end{equation}
A single-path query survives with probability $p^2$, and each additional route
multiplies the failure probability by $1-p^2$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:r2} is a polynomial in $p$ of fixed degree $2m_{\max}$. It has
no threshold, and its form does not depend on the size of the graph, only on the
distribution of $m$. The giant component, by
contrast, depends on an unbounded number of edges and can become singular as
$N\to\infty$. Our experiments remove a fixed number of edges rather than each
edge independently. This changes Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2} only at order $1/E$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:r2} is exact, and this is why theory and simulation agree to
Monte Carlo precision. In reliability theory, $R_L$ for one query is the
source--terminal diameter-constrained reliability. It can be computed in
polynomial time for diameter two, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2}, and it is NP-hard
for diameter three or more \cite{canale2014diameter}, which is why longer
horizons need approximations. The agreement confirms our code and assumptions;
it is not evidence for a model of PrimeKG. We use Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2} as a
baseline: whenever a measured $R_2$ departs from it, an assumption has failed.

Applied to two sets of pairs, Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2} also fixes how well a two-hop
answer separates them. The ratio of the two success rates runs from
$\Pr(m\ge1)$ in one set over that in the other at $f=0$ to the ratio of the
mean redundancies $\langle m\rangle$ as $f\to1$, because
$1-(1-p^2)^m\approx mp^2$ for small $p$. A set whose pairs have more redundant
routes therefore becomes relatively more visible as damage grows.

At our presentation we were asked why theory and simulation agreed to the third
decimal, and we could not say. The answer is that Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2} is exact, so
the agreement tests our code, not PrimeKG. We made a similar mistake once more,
presenting a degree-resolved attack formula as a prediction when, for a fixed
ranking, it only restates which queries the attack hits. The size-biased mean
field of Section~\ref{sec:theory-mf} is the version that can fail, and does.


\subsection{Chance paths}
\label{sec:theory-chance}

How many typed paths would a drug and a disease have by chance? In a
configuration model with the same layer degrees, a drug with $k_d$ targets and a
disease with $k_y$ proteins share on average
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_2 = k_d k_y\, c_2,\qquad c_2=\frac{\sum_x k_D(x)\,k_Y(x)}{K_D K_Y}
\label{eq:cm}
\end{equation}
proteins, where $k_D(x)$ and $k_Y(x)$ are the drug and disease degrees of
protein $x$ and $K_D$ and $K_Y$ are the layer totals. Paths with one
protein--protein step add $k_d k_y c_3$ with
$c_3=\sum_x k_D(x)k_x\sum_x k_Y(x)k_x/(K_D K_Y \sum_x k_x)$, where $k_x$ is the
PPI degree, and each further step multiplies the last term by
$\kappa_{\mathrm{PPI}}-1$, where $\kappa_{\mathrm{PPI}}=187$ is
$\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$ of the full graph's PPI layer (189 and 190
for all edges of the sample and of the full graph): a path
enters a protein through one of its edges and can leave through any of the
others. On the full graph $c_3/c_2=24$ and the four-hop term is 186 times the
three-hop one. With $\Lambda_L$ the cumulative expected number of paths of
length at most $L$, a Poisson approximation gives a chance rate of
$1-e^{-\Lambda_L}$.

The Poisson step fails when paths come in bundles, for example many three-hop
paths through the same drug target. A null model that keeps this bundling
holds fixed the drug's real targets and all proteins within $L-2$
protein--protein steps of them, the set $B_{L-2}(d)$ (with $B_0(d)$ the targets), and
gives the disease $k_y$ random protein links, drawn in proportion to the
disease-layer degree. A typed path of length at most $L$ then exists with
probability
\begin{equation}
1-\bigl(1-w_L\bigr)^{k_y},\qquad w_L=\frac{\sum_{x\in B_{L-2}(d)}k_Y(x)}{K_Y},
\label{eq:ball}
\end{equation}
again with no free parameter. Both models predict the rate at which pairs with
no real relation are connected, and the gap between them and real indications
measures how much a path says. A compact measure of that gap follows from the
Poisson picture. If path counts were Poisson and real pairs had $r_L$ times the
chance mean, their success rates would obey
\begin{equation}
1-R_L^{\mathrm{real}}=\bigl(1-R_L^{\mathrm{chance}}\bigr)^{r_L}.
\label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
We use Eq.~\eqref{eq:excess} only to define an effective excess factor $r_L$
from the observed rates, with the decoy rate as $R_L^{\mathrm{chance}}$; it
equals the ratio of mean path counts only for Poisson counts, and we call
$r_L-1$ the excess.

\subsection{Targeted damage}
\label{sec:theory-mf}

In Callaway's treatment \cite{callaway2000network}, a degree attack makes the
survival of a node depend on its degree: a node of degree $k$ survives with
probability $\sigma_k$, which is 0 above the attack's cut-off, 1 below it, and
fractional at the cut-off. For the giant component,
$F_0(z)=\sum_k p_k\sigma_k z^k$, $F_1(z)=F_0'(z)/\langle k\rangle$,
$u=1-F_1(1)+F_1(u)$ and $S=F_0(1)-F_0(u)$. For retrieval, we call the
difference between the blind-damage formula, Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2-node}, and the
measured success after an attack of the same size the attack penalty.

To predict the penalty, note that a protein $x$ lies on $k_D(x)k_Y(x)$ of the
graph's drug--protein--disease routes. A route therefore lands on a protein
with probability proportional to that product, a second-order form of the
friendship paradox, and routes are biased towards hubs. If the attack removes
the set $H$, a route is cut with probability $h$, and a query whose endpoints
survive keeps at least one route with probability $\rho$:
\begin{equation}
h=\frac{\sum_{x\in H}k_D(x)k_Y(x)}{\sum_x k_D(x)k_Y(x)},
\qquad
\rho\approx1-G_m(h),
\label{eq:mf}
\end{equation}
where $G_m$ is the generating function of $m$ over the queries whose endpoints
survive and $h$ is computed from the whole graph, not from the queries. This
mean field makes two assumptions that can fail separately: that the routes of a
task are typical routes of the graph (selection), and that the routes of one
query are cut independently (correlation). Section~\ref{sec:hubs-mf}
tests both, on the full graph and on the sample.

\subsection{Longer horizons}
\label{sec:theory-rinf}

Without a hop limit, a query succeeds if its drug's surviving targets and its
disease's surviving proteins are connected through surviving PPI edges. While
the PPI layer has a giant component, this holds almost whenever both endpoints
keep a link into it. Weighting each link by the probability $\pi_x=1-u^{k_x}$
that its protein of PPI degree $k_x$ lies in the PPI giant component, with $u$
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:gf-bond}, and treating the two endpoints as independent gives
an attachment approximation for $R_\infty$; on the sample it is within 0.009 of
simulation at every damage level. A simpler form follows from the cut structure.
In a dense core the minimum cut between a drug and a disease is the smaller
endpoint degree $\lambda$ (true for all 200 queries of the sample, mostly the
drug's), and while the PPI giant component is intact a query fails essentially
when that endpoint loses every link:
\begin{equation}
R_\infty(p)\approx1-G_\lambda(1-p).
\label{eq:mincut}
\end{equation}

Between $L=2$ and $L=\infty$ lies limited-path percolation
\cite{lopez2007limited}. López \textit{et al.} ask whether two nodes at distance
$\ell_0$ keep a path no longer than $a\ell_0$. In a random graph the number of
paths grows by a factor $\kappa_0-1$ per extra step, with
$\kappa_0=\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle$, so a path of length $a\ell_0$
survives in expectation once $(\kappa_0-1)^{(a-1)\ell_0}p^{a\ell_0}\gtrsim1$. This
gives the threshold
\begin{equation}
\tilde p_c(a)=(\kappa_0-1)^{(1-a)/a}.
\label{eq:lpp}
\end{equation}
Our queries have $\ell_0=2$, so $a=L/2$. With $\kappa_0=189$ the predicted
thresholds are $f=1-\tilde p_c=0.825$ for $L=3$ and $0.927$ for $L=4$. A
finite query set has no sharp transition, so we compare these values with the
damage at which $R_L/R_\infty=1/2$, a convention. Equation~\eqref{eq:lpp}
assumes many longer paths per pair. When the actual numbers $n_2$ and $n_3$ of
two- and three-hop paths of a pair are known, a local alternative treats each
path as independent:
\begin{equation}
R_3(p)\approx\Bigl\langle1-(1-p^2)^{n_2}(1-p^3)^{n_3}\Bigr\rangle.
\label{eq:paths}
\end{equation}
It neglects that paths share edges, in particular the drug's own edges.
