\section{Theory: Survival as Redundancy}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{One principle}
\label{sec:theory-principle}

Consider an object protected by $X$ redundant units. It survives damage if at
least one unit survives. If each unit survives independently with probability
$q$, the object fails with probability $(1-q)^X$. Averaging over objects, with
$G_X(z)=\sum_x \Pr(X=x)\,z^x$ the probability generating function (PGF) of $X$,
gives
\begin{equation}
\text{surviving fraction}=1-G_X(1-q).
\label{eq:principle}
\end{equation}
The low end of the distribution of $X$ controls this expression: objects with
$X=1$ fail with probability $1-q$, while the tail contributes little.
Equation~\eqref{eq:principle} applies to our three observables with different
units, unit survival probabilities and redundancy distributions
(Table~\ref{tab:principle}, Figure~\ref{fig:redundancy}).

\subsection{Connectivity: percolation and its local limit}
\label{sec:theory-gf}

The course derived the giant component of a random graph with degree
distribution $p_k$ from generating functions \cite{newman2001random}. Let
$g_0(z)=\sum_k p_k z^k$, let $g_1(z)=g_0'(z)/g_0'(1)$, and let $u$ be the
probability that an edge does not lead to the giant component. For edge
retention $p$ and node retention $\phi$,
\begin{align}
u&=1-p+p\,g_1(u), & S_{\mathrm{bond}}&=1-g_0(u), \label{eq:gf-bond}\\
u&=1-\phi+\phi\,g_1(u), & S_{\mathrm{site}}&=\phi\,\bigl[1-g_0(u)\bigr]. \label{eq:gf-site}
\end{align}
A giant component exists only for $p$ above the Molloy--Reed threshold
\cite{molloy1995critical}
\begin{equation}
p_c=\frac{\langle k\rangle}{\langle k^2\rangle-\langle k\rangle},
\label{eq:pc}
\end{equation}
which is 0.0053 for the standard graph and 0.0062 for its PPI layer alone.

The damage we study lies far from this threshold, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gf-bond}
then simplifies. An edge leads to a node that has, on average, $\langle k^2\rangle/\langle k\rangle-1\approx188$ further edges, so almost every surviving edge leads into the giant component. Then $g_1(u)\approx0$, $u\approx1-p$, and
\begin{equation}
S\approx1-g_0(1-p),
\label{eq:s-local}
\end{equation}
which is the fraction of nodes that keep at least one edge. This is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:principle} with the node as the object and its edges as the units.
For $f\le0.8$, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gf-bond} and~\eqref{eq:s-local} differ by at most
0.0012, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:s-local} matches the simulation to within 0.001. Collective
percolation behavior appears only beyond $f\approx0.95$: at $f=0.99$, the
simulated $S$ is 0.112, the full equation gives 0.126, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:s-local}
gives 0.291. Over most of the damage range, connectivity survives for a local
reason: the typical node has many independent edges.

\subsection{Two-hop retrieval: an exact reliability polynomial}
\label{sec:theory-r2}

A two-hop query with $m$ shared proteins $x_1,\dots,x_m$ has $m$ routes
$d\!-\!x_i\!-\!y$. A route needs both of its edges (logical AND), and the query
needs any one route (logical OR). This is a series--parallel network, the
structure Moore and Shannon used to build reliable circuits from unreliable
relays \cite{moore1956reliable}. Routes through different proteins share no edge,
and every entity pair carries one relation (Section~\ref{sec:data-graphs}). When
each edge survives independently with probability $p$, the routes therefore
fail independently, and
\begin{equation}
R_2(p)=1-G_m(1-p^2).
\label{eq:r2}
\end{equation}
For blind node removal, the drug and the disease must survive, and each route
needs only its protein:
\begin{equation}
R_2^{\mathrm{node}}(\phi)=\phi^2\bigl[1-G_m(1-\phi)\bigr].
\label{eq:r2-node}
\end{equation}
In coding terms, a query is an $m$-fold repetition code whose symbols are
two-edge routes. In graph terms, $m$ routes close $m-1$ independent cycles, and
each additional cycle multiplies the failure probability by $1-p^2$. A
single-path query has no cycle, so $R_2=p^2$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:r2} is a polynomial in $p$ of fixed degree $2m_{\max}$. It has
no threshold and does not change as the graph grows. The giant component, by
contrast, depends on an unbounded number of edges and can become singular as
$N\to\infty$. Our experiments remove a fixed number of edges rather than each
edge independently. This changes Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2} only at order $1/E$, with
$E=117{,}891$.

\subsection{Unlimited hops: attaching to the protein network}
\label{sec:theory-rinf}

Without a hop limit, a query succeeds if its drug's surviving targets and its
disease's surviving proteins are connected through surviving PPI edges. While
the PPI layer has a giant component, this holds almost whenever both endpoints
keep at least one link into it. Let $u$ solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:gf-bond} for the PPI
layer. A protein $x$ of PPI degree $k_x$ is then in the PPI giant component with
probability $\pi_x=1-u^{k_x}$. An endpoint $a$ with partner proteins
$\mathcal{N}(a)$ attaches with probability
$A_a=1-\prod_{x\in\mathcal{N}(a)}(1-p\,\pi_x)$, and
\begin{equation}
R_\infty(p)\approx\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{(d,y)\in Q}A_d\,A_y ,
\label{eq:rinf}
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is the set of queries. Equation~\eqref{eq:rinf} treats the two endpoints
as independent and the PPI layer as tree-like, so it is a genuine approximation.

A simpler form follows from the cut structure. By Menger's theorem, the number
of edge-disjoint drug--disease paths equals the minimum edge cut $\lambda$. In
a dense core, $\lambda$ is usually the smaller endpoint degree, and maximum-flow
computation confirms this for all 200 queries. In 89.5\% of queries the drug's degree is smaller than or equal to the disease's.
While the PPI giant component is intact, a query fails essentially when its
weaker endpoint loses every link, so
\begin{equation}
R_\infty(p)\approx1-G_\lambda(1-p),
\label{eq:mincut}
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:rinf} with $\pi_x=1$ and the stronger endpoint assumed
attached. Menger's theorem only guarantees that $\lambda$ counts edge-disjoint
paths, and each such path survives with probability well below $p$. The units of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:principle} here are therefore the weaker endpoint's own edges, not
the paths. Between $L=2$
and $L=\infty$ lies limited-path percolation \cite{lopez2007limited}, which has
no simple closed form. We measure it directly in Section~\ref{sec:blind-horizon}.

\subsection{What is exact, and why agreement can be very close}
\label{sec:theory-exact}

The three predictions should be judged differently.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Equation~\eqref{eq:r2} is exact} for blind damage on a graph where
routes share no edge. When it agrees with simulation to Monte Carlo precision,
that confirms our code and our assumptions. It is not evidence for a model of
PrimeKG, and a visible disagreement would have signaled a bug. We therefore use
it as a baseline: whenever the measured $R_2$ departs from it, an assumption has
failed.
\item \textbf{Equations~\eqref{eq:gf-bond}, \eqref{eq:rinf}
and~\eqref{eq:mincut} are approximations.} They neglect correlations, loops and
the overlap between the two endpoints' neighborhoods. Their agreement with
simulation is a real test, and so are the regimes where they fail.
\item \textbf{The informative case is targeted damage.} In Callaway's
treatment \cite{callaway2000network}, a degree attack does not make units fail
jointly. It makes their survival depend on degree. A node of degree $k$
survives with probability $\sigma_k$, which is 0 above the attack's cut-off
degree, 1 below it, and fractional at the cut-off. For the giant component,
$F_0(z)=\sum_k p_k\sigma_k z^k$, $F_1(z)=F_0'(z)/\langle k\rangle$,
$u=1-F_1(1)+F_1(u)$ and $S=F_0(1)-F_0(u)$. For two-hop retrieval, the same
substitution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2-node} gives
\begin{equation}
R_2^{\mathrm{att}}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{(d,y)\in Q}\sigma_{k_d}\,\sigma_{k_y}
\Bigl[1-\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(1-\sigma_{k_{x_i}}\bigr)\Bigr].
\label{eq:r2-attack}
\end{equation}
For a fixed degree ranking this is almost an identity, because $\sigma_k$ is 0
or 1 except at the cut-off. Its content is that the outcome depends on the
degree of each unit, not on the number of units. We call the difference between
Eq.~\eqref{eq:r2-node} and the measured success the attack penalty. It measures
how badly the assumption of uniform unit survival fails.
\end{itemize}
